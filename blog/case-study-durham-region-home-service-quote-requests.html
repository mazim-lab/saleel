Case Study: How a Durham Region Home Service Business Turned More Clicks Into Quote Requests

Published June 18, 2026 · Saleel

A lot of local businesses have the same problem: the website gets traffic, but the traffic does not turn into enough leads. This case study looks at a Durham Region home service business that wanted more quote requests from Oshawa, Whitby, Ajax, Pickering, and nearby neighbourhoods without rebuilding everything from scratch.

The business was not starting from zero. It already had a working website, a Google Business Profile, and some decent search visibility. The problem was that visitors were landing on the site, skimming for a few seconds, and leaving before they took action. In practice, that meant missed calls, missed quote requests, and a lot of wasted attention.

That is a common issue for small businesses. A website can look fine and still underperform if it does not make the next step obvious. People do not arrive online looking for a design award. They arrive trying to solve a problem quickly. If the site makes them work too hard, they bounce.

What was holding the site back

The original site had three friction points. First, the homepage headline was too generic. It talked about quality and experience, but it did not say what the company actually did in a way that felt immediate. Second, the contact path took too long on mobile. Third, the service pages answered some questions, but not the ones customers asked most often.

That combination is enough to hurt conversions. A visitor might be interested, but interest alone does not produce leads. The site has to translate interest into confidence, and confidence into action, in a very short time.

What changed

The first improvement was the homepage message. We rewrote the headline so it said the service, the area, and the benefit more clearly. That sounds small, but it matters. Local customers want to know in seconds whether they are in the right place.

Next, we simplified the call to action. Instead of scattering attention across too many buttons and links, the site focused on one primary action: request a quote. The button appeared in more places, and the wording was practical rather than clever. Clear beats cute when someone is ready to buy.

We also tightened the mobile experience. The business had a lot of traffic from phones, so the new layout made the phone number easier to tap, the form easier to complete, and the service details easier to scan. That reduced the number of people who meant to inquire but never got around to it.

Small business websites do not need more pages just because more pages feel impressive. They need the right pages, the right message, and a faster path to contact.

How the service pages improved

Instead of treating each service page like a brochure, the content was rebuilt around common customer questions. What areas do you serve? How soon can you help? What affects pricing? What should a customer expect next? Those are the questions that often decide whether someone reaches out.

We also added more local proof. Not fake hype, just credible signals that made the business feel real: service-area language, project examples, and a stronger trust section. For Durham Region buyers, that local context matters. People are more comfortable when they can tell you actually work where they live.

Why the change worked

The biggest win was not one dramatic feature. It was the combination of clearer messaging, less friction, and better alignment with buyer intent. Visitors no longer had to guess what to do next. They could understand the offer quickly, trust the business faster, and move to the quote form without feeling lost.

That is usually how website improvement works for small businesses. A few practical changes can move the needle more than a complete redesign. Better conversion often comes from removing confusion, not adding complexity.

What Durham Region businesses can learn from this

  • Make the homepage say exactly what you do and who you help.
  • Put one main action in front of visitors, especially on mobile.
  • Answer the questions people actually ask before they buy.
  • Use local proof so the business feels familiar and trustworthy.
  • Measure quote requests, calls, and form submissions instead of guessing.

If your website gets some traffic but not enough leads, the issue is usually not a lack of interest. It is a lack of clarity. Most people do not need more convincing. They need fewer obstacles.
