Website ROI for Durham Region Small Businesses: A Simple Cost-Per-Lead Scorecard

Published October 22, 2026 · Saleel

Page views are easy to count, but they do not show whether your website is paying off. A short monthly scorecard connects website activity to qualified leads, customers, and revenue.

A small business website should do more than look professional. It should help customers in Oshawa, Whitby, Ajax, Pickering, Clarington, and across Durham Region take a useful next step. That might be requesting a quote, booking an appointment, calling your team, or completing a purchase.

Website return on investment becomes much easier to understand when you stop treating traffic as the final result. Track the five numbers below each month and you will have a practical scorecard for deciding what to improve.

1. Record your total website cost

Start with every cost required to keep the website working: design or development, hosting, maintenance, software, content, search optimization, and paid campaigns that send visitors to the site. For a one-time project, divide the cost across a reasonable period, such as 24 or 36 months, so one month is not distorted.

For example, if your allocated website cost is $250 per month and you spend $500 on search ads, your monthly investment is $750. Use the same approach each month so comparisons remain meaningful.

2. Count qualified website leads

A lead is not simply any form submission. Decide what “qualified” means for your business. A Durham contractor might require a project inside the service area and above a minimum size. A clinic might count a completed appointment request. An online store can use completed orders instead.

Track calls, forms, bookings, chat requests, and purchases that began on the website. Add a “How did you hear about us?” question when attribution is unclear, and make sure analytics record completed actions rather than button clicks alone.

Cost per qualified lead = total monthly website and campaign cost ÷ qualified website leads. If $750 produces 15 qualified leads, the cost per qualified lead is $50.

3. Measure the lead-to-customer rate

Not every qualified inquiry becomes a customer. Divide new customers from website leads by the number of qualified leads. If 15 qualified leads create five customers, your close rate is about 33 per cent.

This number helps separate website problems from sales-process problems. If lead volume is healthy but the close rate is low, review response time, pricing clarity, follow-up, and whether your marketing is attracting the right audience. If the close rate is strong but volume is low, improve search visibility and conversion opportunities.

4. Estimate customer value

Use average first-purchase value for a conservative view, or average customer lifetime value when repeat business is dependable. A $300 first job may lead to years of maintenance work, while a one-time purchase may have little repeat value. Choose one definition and document it.

Multiply customers won through the website by the value you selected. Five new customers worth an average of $600 each represent $3,000 in attributed value for the month.

5. Calculate a simple website ROI

Subtract the website investment from attributed value, divide by the investment, and multiply by 100. Using the examples above, the calculation is ($3,000 − $750) ÷ $750 × 100, for an estimated website ROI of 300 per cent.

This estimate does not need to be perfect to be useful. Apply the same rules consistently and compare trends over several months. Seasonal businesses should compare against the same period last year, not only the previous month.

Turn the scorecard into action

Add one diagnostic number to your scorecard: the percentage of visitors who complete a key action. Then improve the weakest stage of the journey. Low conversion may call for clearer service pages, faster mobile performance, stronger proof, or a shorter form. High lead costs may point to weak search targeting. A low close rate may require faster follow-up.

Review the scorecard monthly, annotate major changes, and avoid judging an improvement after only a few days. The goal is not to chase every metric. It is to connect your website to business outcomes and invest where the evidence is strongest.
